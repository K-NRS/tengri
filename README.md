```sh
npx tengri
```

A random line from the Orkhon inscriptions or Kutadgu Bilig in a small frame: Old Turkic script, Latin transcription, modern Turkish and English. It opens with a short look at the Tengri symbol (any key skips it). Then press space or → for another line, ← to go back, **t** for the tamga screensaver (any key brings you back), q to quit; the last line stays on screen. Piped, it prints a single random line.

Write your own:

```sh
npx tengri "kök türk"      # 𐰚𐰇𐰚⁚𐱅𐰇𐰼𐰚
```

Your terminal needs a font with the Old Turkic block (U+10C00–10C4F). Without one you see empty boxes (the Latin line under each quote still reads fine). On macOS: `brew install --cask font-noto-sans-old-turkic`, then restart the terminal.

## Library

```js
import { transliterate, randomQuote, quotes } from 'tengri'

transliterate('tengri') // 𐱅𐰭𐰼𐰃
randomQuote()           // { source, ref, script, text, tr, en }
```

Zero dependencies, Node 18+.

## Tamgas and motifs

```sh
npx tengri --tamga
```

A slow, full-screen screensaver on the terminal's alternate screen, centred and redrawn to your window (resize it and it follows). It opens with the Tengrist symbol, then nine Turkic tamgas and six kilim motifs, drawn in braille, wipe in (diagonally, then from the centre, alternating), glow while they are held and dissolve, one after another and then around again. Each takes about ten seconds. Any key stops it and brings your terminal back as it was.

The drawings come in sizes and the biggest one that fits the window is used (the emblem up to 96×48 characters, the rest up to 96×32). The smallest needs 52 columns by 20 rows; below that, or when piped, each one is printed once.

## The first word

![The Kül Tigin stele, Göktürk Museum, Khashaat, Mongolia](docs/kul-tigin-stele.jpg)

*The Kül Tigin stele (732), Orkhon Valley, Mongolia. Photo: [Vezirtonyukuk](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kultigin_Monument_of_Orkhon_Inscriptions.jpeg), CC BY-SA 4.0, resized.*

In 1889 Nikolai Yadrintsev's expedition found two huge stone steles in Mongolia's Orkhon Valley, set up in the early 8th century for the brothers Kül Tigin and Bilge Kagan. They were covered in an alphabet nobody could read, and in places in Chinese. Nobody was even sure what language it wrote.

In Copenhagen the linguist Vilhelm Thomsen went to work on the printed copies (the Finnish expedition's edition and Radloff's atlas, both 1892). He had 38 signs and no key. A sign that kept turning up between two others had to be a vowel, and he found three of those. The consonants fell into two sets, one for back vowels and one for front vowels: vowel harmony, the fingerprint of a Turkic language.

Then he went hunting for words, and in his own account of it (his French notice of 1893) the first one he caught was *tengri*. One word was everywhere, alone or with endings, often opening a new section and in what looked like the khan's title, "heavenly kagan". It ended in the vowel he had just identified as *i*. Mongolian and every Turkic language share a word like that: *tengri*, sky, god.

The second word was the prince's name. The Chinese text gives something like Kiueh-ti-kin, and everyone read it as Kök-tigin, "blue prince". Thomsen doubted it for a long time. The sign he had taken for *k* turned out to be *l*, and the name was Kül Tigin; the Chinese had simply dropped the final *l*, as it does in *pi-kia*, the Turkic *bilge*, "wise". Those two words gave him the third, the one on every line: *Türk*, the Chinese Tou-kiue. The language was Turkic.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| 𐱅𐰭𐰼𐰃 | tengri | sky, god |
| 𐰚𐰇𐰠⁚𐱅𐰃𐰏𐰤 | Kül Tigin | the prince the stele was raised for |
| 𐱅𐰇𐰼𐰚 | Türk | the people |

On 15 December 1893 he announced it to the Royal Danish Academy of Sciences and Letters (some sources say 25 November). That date is now World Turkic Language Family Day. Wilhelm Radloff's reading of the texts followed in 1894–95, using Thomsen's key, and Thomsen's full account came out in 1896 (*Inscriptions de l'Orkhon déchiffrées*). His 1893 notice is [on the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/dechiffrementdesinscriptionsdelorkhonetdelienissei).

That is why this package is called `tengri`.

## How the transliteration works

`transliterate` is a heuristic over Turkish spelling or scholarly Old Turkic transcription (ä ï ŋ š γ q work too). It follows the main conventions of the Orkhon stones:

- consonants `b d g k l n r s t y` have a back-vowel and a front-vowel letter; the nearest vowel picks one
- `a`/`e` are written only at the end of a word; of `ı i o u ö ü` only the first and a final one are written
- words are separated by `⁚` (U+205A), as on the stones
- syllable signs and ligatures (𐰸 𐰜 𐰦 𐰡 …) are never emitted

It spells *tengri*, *bodun*, *asra* as the stones do. It will not match every carved spelling, since the stones themselves vary. Treat the output as a reading aid, not an edition.

## Quotes and sources

`data/quotes.json` holds 15 short passages: 10 from the Kül Tigin inscription (with the Bilge Kagan parallels) and 5 couplets of the *Kutadgu Bilig* (Yusuf Has Hacib, 1069).

- The 8th–11th century texts are public domain. The transcriptions are simplified Latin spellings, cross-checked against the Turkish Wikisource editions of the inscriptions and published studies (Ayaz 2021, Bilgin 2005, Mert & Albayrak 2013). Where editions disagree, the quote's `note` says so.
- The Turkish and English renderings are my own, not taken from any published translation. They are not scholarly translations. Corrections are welcome.
- Line labels: E, S, N = east, south, north face. `Tengri teg Tengride…` is KT S 1 / BK N 1.
- *Kutadgu Bilig* is written in Karakhanid Turkic with Arabic letters, never in Göktürk script. The Old Turkic letters shown for it are a **stylization**: the same rules as above applied to the Latin text, nothing a scribe ever wrote. The data marks it as `script: "stylized"`. Couplet numbers follow the common Arat-based numbering; the Fergana manuscript numbers differ (211 is 208 there).

## Ornaments

Tamgas have no Unicode block, so the frame uses the Old Turkic word separator (⁚) as its only ornament and the animation draws tamgas and motifs as braille bitmaps.

The emblem at the top, which also opens `--tamga`, is the Tengrist symbol, rendered to braille from [Tengrism1.svg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tengrism1.svg) (vector by Modanung, CC0; the design is credited on Commons to tengry.org, marked public domain).
